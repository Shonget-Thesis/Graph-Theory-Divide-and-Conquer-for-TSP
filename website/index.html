[01] Problem and objectiverubric · 10 pts

Visit every stop once.
Come back.

A one-day city tour of downtown Cagayan de Oro that starts and ends at USTP, as a Traveling Salesperson Problem.

suggested time · 2–3 min
[01.1] The Traveling Salesperson Problemminimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle

The shortest loop through every place.

  • Visit every required location exactly once and return to the start, at the smallest total cost.
  • In graph terms: in a weighted graph, find a Hamiltonian cycle of minimum total weight.
  • The decision version is NP-complete [5]. Exact methods blow up: brute force is O(n!) and Held–Karp dynamic programming is O(n²2ⁿ) [3].
  • That is why there are fast heuristics, which give up the guarantee of optimality for speed [6]. Ours is Divide and Conquer, Algorithm 2.8 of Luna-Garcia, Candano & Caga-anan (2024) [1], one of the eight classical algorithms they compare.
Growth of the exact methodsn = 10
brute force
(n−1)!/2 = 181,440 distinct tours
Held–Karp
n²·2ⁿ ≈ 102,400 steps
D&C
O(n²), well under a millisecond here

At n = 10 the exact optimum is still cheap, so we compute it, but only to check our heuristic.

[01.2] Our real-life problemtourist itinerary · Cagayan de Oro City

A guest tour of downtown CDO.

Situation. The university hosts visiting guests (for example, delegates from a partner school) and takes them on a one-day city tour by service vehicle. The tour leaves the , stops once at each of nine landmarks in downtown Cagayan de Oro, and returns to the campus at the end of the day. The planners want the order of stops with the shortest total driving distance. That saves fuel and leaves more time at each stop.

Area. Downtown Cagayan de Oro City, Misamis Oriental: the city centre and nearby barangays (Lapasan, Licoan, Burgos, Nazareth, Carmen and Macabalan), roughly 2.5 × 2.2 km.

Start / end: V0, . Required stops: V1–V9.

Pins: OpenStreetMap / Nominatim coordinates · map © OpenStreetMap contributors
Objectiveminimise · km

Minimise the total travel distance in kilometres:

w(u,v) = road distance between u and v · n =

Assumptions4
  1. Travel by car. Between two stops the driver follows the route OSRM's default fastest car profile returns on the public road network.
  2. The distance is the same in both directions. Where one-way streets make the data differ, we average the two directions.
  3. Traffic, opening hours, waiting and time at each stop are not modelled. They do not change the driving distance.
  4. The vehicle has no capacity or fuel limit within one day.
Constraints2
  1. Start and end at V0.
  2. Visit every one of V1–V9 exactly once.

In graph terms the route must be a Hamiltonian cycle of the graph in 03.

[02] The Divide and Conquer algorithmrubric · 20 pts

Split the map.
Solve the halves.
Stitch.

Algorithm 2.8 of Luna-Garcia et al. (2024) [1], with one correction to its merge step.

suggested time · 2–3 min
[02.1] The proceduredivide · conquer · combine [4]

Divide the problem into smaller subproblems, solve each recursively (the smallest directly), and combine the sub-solutions. For the TSP, the locations are split geometrically, a short tour is built for each half, and the two tours are joined through the cheapest connection, the bridge. Partitioning the plane and joining local tours goes back to Karp [2].

  1. Step 1
    Shuffle

    Randomise the order of the cities. Our split sorts by a deterministic coordinate-and-index key, so the shuffle does not change the splits. We keep it to follow the paper.

  2. Step 2
    Divide

    Pick the x axis (longitude) or the y axis (latitude) at random, sort, and cut at the median. Halves have sizes ⌊m/2⌋ and ⌈m/2⌉. This axis choice is the randomness that changes the result.

  3. Step 3
    Base cases

    1 city ⇒ length 0. 2 cities a, b ⇒ the tour a → b → a, of length 2·w(a,b), as in the paper.

  4. Step 4
    Conquer

    Solve both halves recursively, giving the cycles T₁ and T₂.

  5. Step 5
    Combine

    Find the shortest bridge (a, b), with a in T₁ and b in T₂, and join the two cycles through it.

  6. Step 6
    Adjust

    Make sure no city repeats: the result must be one cycle through every city of the group exactly once.

ComplexityTheorem 2.8

Sorting for the split costs O(n log n). The bridge search compares all n/2 · n/2 cross pairs, O(n²). The loop over edge pairs of the two cycles skips most pairs, but is still O(n²) in the worst case. Building the merged list is O(n). The merge cost halves at each level, so the total is a geometric series [4, 8].

PseudocodeAlgorithm 2.8 restated
DC-TSP(C):
  if |C| ≤ 2: return base tour of C
  axis ← random choice of {x, y}
  L, R ← cut C at the median of axis
  T₁ ← DC-TSP(L);  T₂ ← DC-TSP(R)
  (a, b) ← shortest bridge, a ∈ T₁, b ∈ T₂
  return MERGE(T₁, T₂, a, b)   ▷ each city once
[02.2] The merge step, and a fix to the paper's codeAppendix 4.8 of [1]

The printed merge repeats cities.

[02.3] Advantages, limitations, optimalityheuristic

    

      

        
Advantages+

        
    
          
  • Fast and scalable: O(n²), against O(n!) for brute force and O(n²2ⁿ) for Held–Karp. In the paper's experiments its average running time was far below PSO and the genetic algorithm at every size tested [1].
    • 
          
  • Simple and local: each subproblem only involves nearby cities, so it is easy to follow by hand, and the two halves could run in parallel.
    • 
          
  • Uses geography: cities close together tend to be visited one after another, which suits dense clusters like downtown CDO.
    • 
        

      

      

        
Limitations−

        
    
          
  • No optimality guarantee: each merge is greedy and never revisited. In the paper, Divide and Conquer had the highest average tour length of all heuristics on the 128- and 256-city instances [1].
    • 
          
  • Depends on random choices: different axis choices give different tours.
    • 
          
  • Needs coordinates: it splits by map position, but costs are road distances. Places close on the map can be far apart by road (rivers, one-way streets).
    • 
          
  • Boundary effects: a median cut can separate two very close cities, forcing extra travel across the cut.
    • 
        

      

      

        
Optimal or approximate?approximate

        
Divide and Conquer is a heuristic. It always returns a valid tour, but it does not guarantee an optimal one, and we found no constant worst-case approximation ratio for it.

        
On our own instance the route it finds ( km) is longer than the exact optimum computed by Held–Karp ( km). See 05.

      [05.1] Interpreting the routedoes it meet the objective?
      
    
      
      
      
        
      The route meets the constraints. The guests leave USTP, visit each of the nine landmarks exactly once, and return to USTP, in one loop of  km. The loop goes south to Limketkai, north to the port, then south-west through Capitol University, Centrio, Pelaez, Museo de Oro, Divisoria and the Cathedral, and finally north to Liceo before heading back east to USTP.
      
        
      Because the divide step groups nearby places, the tight cluster around Divisoria (V4, V5, V6) is visited one after another, using short edges ( and  km).
      
        
      Summing the raw one-way OSRM values in the order driven gives  km. The difference from  km comes from the model's averaged, undirected weights.
      
      
      
      
      
        
      
          
      Where the route loses to the optimummerge M2
      
          
      Only one thing differs from the optimal tour: the order of the stops inside this cluster.
      
          
      
          
      Merge M1 only builds the triangle V3, V4, V5, whose length does not depend on the order. Merge M2 decides it. There the shortest bridge is V6–V5 ( km), and the paper's rule must add it. The cheapest way to open the triangle around it removes V3–V5, leaving V3 → V4 → V5 (Δ =  km).
      
          
      The optimal order needs V6–V4 ( km), which is not the shortest bridge, so it is never considered. Opening the triangle at V3–V4 and adding V6–V4 and V8–V3 would give Δ =  km, which is cheaper, but only the best-splice variant can choose it.
      
          
      The long final leg V8 → V0 ( km) is not the cause: the optimal tour uses the same edge. The gap is a greedy, irreversible merge decision.

      [05.3] Limitations and improvementspossible next steps
      
    
      
      
      
        
      Limitations of the solution
      
        
        
          
      • The route is approximate (% above optimal) and depends on the seed: over  seeds it ranges from  to  km.
        • 
          
      • Distance is the only cost. Traffic, travel time, opening hours and time at each stop are ignored, though they matter for a real one-day tour.
        • 
          
      • Weights are symmetric averages, so one-way streets are only approximated (the route is  km on the raw one-way values).
        • 
          
      • Road distances come from OpenStreetMap routing. Google Maps gave  km against  km (+%) for the final route. The optimality claim holds for the OSRM matrix only; we did not recompute the optimum with Google distances.
        • 
        
      
      
      
      
      
        
      Improvements
      
        
        
          
      • Local search after merging [6]. A single 2-opt move that reverses V4 → V5 replaces V3–V4 () and V5–V6 () with V3–V5 () and V4–V6 (). That saves  km and gives exactly  km, the optimum. Shown as "2-opt fix" on the map above.
        • 
          
      • Multiple restarts. Run several seeds and keep the shortest. The best of  runs was  km. We only know it is the optimum because Held–Karp says so; restarts alone prove nothing.
        • 
          
      • Merge rule. The best-splice variant reached the optimum more often ( vs.  runs), but its mean was  km against  km. A cheaper merge at each step does not guarantee a shorter final tour.
        • 
          
      • Richer weights. Travel time with traffic from Google Maps, or a directed matrix, if the objective changes from distance to time.
