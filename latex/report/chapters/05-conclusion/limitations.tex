\subsection{Limitations and Possible Improvements}\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Limitations of the solution.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The route is approximate (\DCGap\% above optimal) and depends on the random seed.
  \item Distance is the only cost. Traffic, travel time, opening hours and the time spent at each stop
        are ignored, although they matter for a real one-day tour.
  \item Weights are made symmetric by averaging, so one-way streets are only approximated.
  \item Road distances come from OpenStreetMap routing; Google Maps may give slightly different values.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Improvements.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local search after merging.} Applying 2-opt or Or-opt moves to the final tour removes
        crossings and long ``seam'' edges such as V8--V0, and usually closes most of the remaining
        gap~\cite{applegate2006tsp}.
  \item \textbf{Multiple restarts.} Run several seeds and keep the shortest tour. Each run takes well
        under a millisecond here, and the best of \RunCount{} runs was \BridgeBest{}~km.
  \item \textbf{Merge rule.} We also tested the best-splice rule, which may break any edge of either
        sub-tour rather than only edges next to the shortest bridge. On this instance it reached the
        optimum more often (\BestRuleOptHits{} vs.\ \BridgeOptHits{} runs) but its mean was
        \BestRuleMean{}~km against \BridgeMean{}~km (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}). A cheaper merge at
        each step does not guarantee a shorter final tour, because every merge is still a local,
        irreversible decision.
  \item \textbf{Richer weights.} Use travel time with traffic from Google Maps, or a directed (asymmetric)
        matrix, if the objective changes from distance to time.
  \item \textbf{Exact methods for small instances.} For about 15 stops or fewer, Held--Karp is fast
        enough to give the true optimum. Divide and Conquer is most useful when $n$ is too large for that.
\end{itemize}
